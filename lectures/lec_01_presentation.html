Options come one by one, and there is no going back

Question: Does anybody know what optimal stopping is?

  • Optimal stopping is the problem of choosing the best option
  • from a sequence of options
  • that are revealed one by one

Topic: understanding optimal stopping problems and the famous Secretary Problem. Why this matters: optimal stopping is everywhere in life, from hiring decisions to finding apartments to choosing when to sell stocks. Today we learn the mathematical strategy that maximizes the chance of making the best choice.

The Secretary Problem: hire now or keep looking?

  • You are hiring a secretary and interview candidates one by one
  • After each interview: hire or continue searching
  • Once you reject a candidate, you cannot go back
  • How to maximize the chance of hiring the best candidate?

The name comes from the 1960s. The problem is about finding the best candidate, not about secretaries. It is also known as the marriage problem or the dowry problem.

The setup in three lines

  • We have n candidates and interview them one by one
  • After each one: hire or continue searching
  • We can only rank candidates relative to those we have already seen (ordinal ranking)

Question: Where in your life can you say “this one is better than that one”, but not “this one is worth 7 out of 10”?

Ordinal ranking: we can say which of two candidates is better, but we do not have a score or an absolute scale. This is what makes the problem hard, we never know how good the best candidate could be.

Two ways to fail

Question: Any idea how we could fail?

  1. Reject everyone and never hire: stopping too late
  2. Hire someone too early: stopping too early

Look at the first 37%, then leap

  1. Look at the first 37% of candidates without hiring anyone
  2. Remember the best candidate so far
  3. Leap: hire the next candidate who is better than that one
  4. This finds the very best candidate in 37% of all cases

This is the mathematically optimal strategy. It means we still fail 63% of the time, but this is the best we can possibly do. The 37% holds for a large number of candidates; with only a few candidates, the chance is even higher.

Picking at random gets worse with every candidate

Chance of the best candidate if we pick one at random:

  • 1 candidate: 100%, 2 candidates: 50%, 3 candidates: 33%
  • 4 candidates: 25%, 5 candidates: 20%

Question: Do you see the pattern?

Pattern: 1/n. Look-and-leap never drops below 37%, however many candidates there are.

Random choice gets worse with every extra candidate, because each individual candidate is less likely to be the best. The look-and-leap strategy does not have this problem: its success chance never drops below 37%, however many candidates there are, and with few candidates it is even higher.

Why 37%?

  • Look at the first share \(x\) of candidates, then leap
  • Chance of getting the best: about \(-x \ln x\)1
  • In words: look longer and your benchmark improves, but the best may already be gone
  • Largest at \(x = 1/e \approx 37\%\)2

  1. Where does this come from? You win if the best candidate comes after your look phase and nobody before them beats your benchmark. That happens exactly when the best of all earlier candidates was in your look phase. Adding up these chances for every position, with many candidates, gives about \(-x \ln x\), where \(x\) is the share of candidates you only look at.

  2. To find the best look phase, we only need to know where \(-x \ln x\) reaches its maximum. Setting its derivative \(-\ln x - 1\) to zero gives \(x = 1/e \approx 37\%\), where \(e \approx 2.718\) is the base of the natural logarithm. For the full story, see Ferguson, T.S. (1989) ‘Who solved the secretary problem?’, Statistical Science, 4(3).

Let the computer try it out

  1. Shuffle 100 candidates into a random order
  2. Look at the first 37 and remember the best one
  3. Hire the first candidate who beats that benchmark
  4. Check: did we get the very best?
  5. Repeat 10,000 times and count how often we won

The same idea in code

import random
random.seed(42)

def one_round(n=100, look=37):
    candidates = list(range(n))          # n - 1 is the best
    random.shuffle(candidates)
    benchmark = max(candidates[:look])   # best of the look phase
    for c in candidates[look:]:
        if c > benchmark:                # first one who beats it
            return c == n - 1            # did we hire the best?
    return False                         # nobody beat the benchmark

wins = sum(one_round() for _ in range(10_000))
print(f"Best candidate found in {wins / 10_000:.1%} of rounds")

Real life changes the assumptions

So far, we made some strong simplifications. Real life is messier.

Question: What could be different in real life?

  • The other person can say no
  • You can go back to someone you passed
  • You know how good an option is, not just how it compares
  • You have a fixed time, not a fixed number of options
  • One failure can mean losing everything
  • Looking costs time and effort

If you can be rejected, leap earlier

Question: In dating, the other person can also say no. When to leap?

  • 50% chance of rejection: leap at 25%
  • Formula: \(q^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\), \(q\) = chance that your offer is accepted1
  • In words: the more often you hear “no”, the earlier you leap

Life lesson: higher risk of rejection means we should be less picky. With q = 0.5 the formula gives 0.5 to the power of 2, that is 25%. If offers are always accepted (q close to 1), the formula gives back the familiar 1/e.

Example from Brian Christian: searching from age 18 to 40, the 37% rule says get serious at 26.1. With many rejections, start at 23.5.

  1. How it works: after the look phase, you ask everyone who beats all earlier candidates, and each says yes with chance \(q\). You get the best only if the best says yes and everyone you asked before said no. More rejections mean you use up more candidates after the look phase, so you have to start earlier. The stated formula is the best look phase, for example 25% when \(q = 50\%\). As in the basic problem, your chance of getting the best equals the look phase. With \(q\) close to 1, it gives back 37%.

If you can go back, wait longer

Question: What if you could return to someone you passed on, but they might say no?

  • Going back works only half the time: look at 61%1, then leap
  • If nobody better comes, return to the best you passed
  • In words: a second chance makes it safe to look longer

Example from the book: an immediate offer is always accepted, a belated one only with 50%. The safety net of going back shifts the look phase from 37% to 61%.

  1. Christian, B. & Griffiths, T. (2016). Algorithms to Live By. Henry Holt, Chapter 1.

If you know the scale, use a threshold

Question: What if you know how good each option is, not just how it compares?

  • Example: you know test scores, or the price per square meter of a flat
  • No look phase needed: take the first option above a threshold
  • The threshold drops as fewer options remain
  • Chance of getting the very best rises to 58%1

This is the “full information” version. With absolute scores and a known distribution, you don’t need to learn a benchmark first. The threshold starts high and falls as you run out of options.

  1. Gilbert, J.P. & Mosteller, F. (1966). Recognizing the maximum of a sequence. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 61(313), 35–73.

Fixed time instead of a fixed number of options

Example: one year to find a flat, viewings come as they come

Question: How should we adapt our strategy?

  • Same principle: look for the first 37% of the time1, then leap
  • The number of options is not fixed: the harder we search, the more options we see

This holds if options arrive evenly over time. We can also decide how intensively we search, for example by viewing more flats per week. Searching harder in the look phase improves the benchmark.

  1. Christian, B. & Griffiths, T. (2016). Algorithms to Live By. Henry Holt, Chapter 1.

The burglar: knowing when to quit

Example: each break-in succeeds with chance \(q\), one failure means losing everything

Question: When should the burglar retire?

  • Stop after about \(\frac{q}{1-q}\) successes
  • With \(q = 90\%\): retire after 9 break-ins1
  • Same logic for any streak that can end badly: a winning run in the casino, a project that keeps growing in risk

  1. Ferguson, T.S. Optimal Stopping and Applications. Lecture notes, University of California, Los Angeles, https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/. The rule assumes the same haul every time: stop once your total haul is worth more than what you would expect to gain from one more attempt.

In practice, people stop too early

  • In about a dozen experiments, people leap earlier than the 37% rule suggests1
  • Question: Why might that be?
  • Searching costs time and effort, and the rule ignores that
  • If looking is expensive, leaping earlier can be the right choice

Experiments by Seale and Rapoport (1997). The 37% rule assumes that looking is free. In real life, every viewing or interview costs time, so stopping a bit earlier is often rational, not a mistake.

Side note for drivers: an increase in occupancy from 90 to 95% doubles the search time for all drivers. Each variation changes the rule a little, but the structure stays: options come one at a time, and you cannot go back.

  1. Christian, B. & Griffiths, T. (2016). Algorithms to Live By. Henry Holt, Chapter 1.

What we covered

The problem: choosing the best when options come one at a time

Many decisions share one structure: options arrive one at a time, and there is no going back.

The rule: look first, then leap

Looking at the first 37% and then taking the first better option gives the best possible chance of picking the very best: 37%.

Real life: when the assumptions change

The rule adapts to real life: leap earlier if you can be rejected or looking is costly, wait longer if you can go back.
